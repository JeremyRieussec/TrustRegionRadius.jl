\title{A small cap shrinks the region until the method is gradient descent}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
$\Rgrad(\omega,\overline{\mu})$ bounds the multiplier by $\overline{\mu}$, which
the asymptotic theory needs. The radius $\Delta_k = \mu_k\norm{g_k}$ is then
small everywhere, and a small region makes the linear term of the model dominate
its quadratic term. We measure the resulting degeneration on a quartic with four
known critical points, over \NConfigs\ configurations. With truncated conjugate
gradient the degeneration is exact and total below $\overline{\mu} = 0.1$, where
every step is the Cauchy point. With the exact Mor\'e-Sorensen solver it is
asymptotic, and we fit its rate. We also reconcile two Cauchy statistics that
disagree, and the reconciliation refutes the explanation we set out to confirm.
\end{abstract}

% =============================================================================
\section{The claim}\label{sec: claim}
% =============================================================================

A cap on the multiplier holds the radius down. The model is
\begin{equation}
  m_k(s) = f(x_k) + g_k^{\top}s + \tfrac12 s^{\top}H_k s ,
  \label{eqn: model}
\end{equation}
and on a region of radius $\Delta$ the linear term is $\calO(\Delta)$ while the
quadratic term is $\calO(\Delta^2)$. As $\Delta \to 0$ the linear term dominates
and the minimiser of \eqref{eqn: model} turns towards $-g_k$. The model Hessian
stops influencing the direction. The same happens to $\Rdfo(\omega)$ for small
$\zeta$, which holds $\Delta_k$ below $\zeta\omega_k$.

The two subsolvers degenerate differently, and the difference is the point of
this report. Truncated conjugate gradient starts from $s = 0$ with first
direction $-g_k$. If the region truncates it there, the returned step is
$-\Delta_k g_k/\norm{g_k}$ and the cosine is one to machine precision. The exact
Mor\'e-Sorensen solver returns $s^* = -(H_k + \sigma I)^{-1}g_k$ with $\sigma$
set so that the step reaches the boundary. As $\Delta \to 0$ we have
$\sigma \to \infty$ and $s^* \to -\Delta g_k/\norm{g_k}$, without ever equalling
it.

\section{Setup}\label{sec: setup}
% =============================================================================

The test function is
\begin{equation}
  f(p) = p_1^4 - p_1^3 + \left(\tfrac14 - \tfrac{p_1}{2}\right)p_2^2
         + \tfrac{p_2^4}{4} .
  \label{eqn: f}
\end{equation}
It has four critical points of three kinds. The origin is degenerate, with
$\nabla^2 f$ singular and positive semidefinite, eigenvalues $0$ and $1/2$. The
point $(0.75, 0)$ is a non-degenerate saddle with $\lambda_{\min} = -0.25$. The
two local minimisers sit at $((1+\sqrt5)/4, \pm\sqrt{(\sqrt5-1)/4})$.

We sweep three axes. The cap $\overline{\mu}$ takes \NCaps\ values from $0.01$ to
$128$, and $\zeta$ takes \NZetas\ values from $0.1$ to $20$. The subsolver is
truncated conjugate gradient or exact Mor\'e-Sorensen. The model Hessian is
exact, SR1 with memory ten, or L-BFGS with memory five. Every run starts from
$x_0 = (-0.5, 0.6)$ and goes through \texttt{tr\_solve}.

\section{Three classes of step}\label{sec: classes}
% =============================================================================

A single Cauchy percentage merges two different events. We separate them.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \emph{Truncated at the boundary}: $\norm{s_k} = \Delta_k$ with direction
        $-g_k$. The Hessian has touched neither the direction nor the length.
        This alone is gradient descent in disguise.
  \item \emph{Stopped inside after one CG iteration}: the direction is $-g_k$
        and the Hessian sets the length through the exact line search on the
        model.
  \item \emph{Two or more CG iterations}.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab: classes} reports the three fractions. Figure~\ref{fig: classes}
shows them stacked.

The degeneration is total below $\overline{\mu} = 0.1$. With the exact Hessian
at $\overline{\mu} \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.1\}$ every iteration falls in class one,
and the minimum cosine over the whole run is one. The method is exactly gradient
descent for two thousand iterations. Class two is almost never occupied, which
says the degeneration proceeds by truncation rather than by a short interior
step.

\section{Two Cauchy statistics, reconciled}\label{sec: cauchy}
% =============================================================================

The package reports \texttt{cauchy\_fraction}, the fraction of iterations with
$\cos(s_k,-g_k)$ within $10^{-12}$ of one. Counting \texttt{cg\_iters == 1}
tests the Krylov dimension instead. The two disagree at $\overline{\mu} = 4$,
where they read \CauchyFour\ and \CgOneFour, and at $\overline{\mu} = 8$, where
they read \CauchyEight\ and \CgOneEight. The cosine test counts more.

The natural explanation is that $g_k$ is close to an eigenvector of $H_k$. The
second CG direction would then add nothing to the direction while still being
counted. On this function the iterates approach the axis $p_2 = 0$, where the
Hessian is diagonal and the gradient is an eigenvector, so the explanation is
plausible.

\emph{We tested it and it is wrong.} We recorded
$\sin\angle(H_kg_k, g_k)$ at every iteration, which vanishes exactly when $g_k$
is an eigenvector of $H_k$, and compared the disagreeing iterations against the
agreeing ones. At $\overline{\mu} = 4$ the medians are \SinDisFour\ and
\SinAgreeFour, which is no difference at all, and both sit near $1/\sqrt2$,
far from an eigenvector. At $\overline{\mu} = 8$ they are \SinDisEight\ and
\SinAgreeEight, so the disagreeing iterations are \emph{further} from the
eigenvector case, not closer.

The mechanism is the boundary crossing. Let $z_1$ denote the first Steihaug
iterate, the exact minimiser of \eqref{eqn: model} along $-g_k$. Truncated CG
stops at the first iteration when $\norm{z_1} \geq \Delta_k$ and continues when
it does not. On the disagreeing iterations $\norm{z_1}/\Delta_k$ has median
\ZDisFour\ at $\overline{\mu} = 4$, so $z_1$ lands within a part in $10^{8}$ of
the boundary. Conjugate gradient does not truncate, takes a second iteration,
and immediately backtracks to the boundary by a negligible distance. The step is
on the boundary and parallel to $-g_k$ to within $10^{-12}$, and
\texttt{cg\_iters} records two. On the agreeing iterations the same ratio has
median \ZAgreeFour. All \NDisFour\ disagreeing iterations exceed $0.999$, against
a small minority of the agreeing ones.

The two statistics answer different questions, and neither is wrong. The cosine
test measures the direction that came back. The Krylov count measures the work
that produced it. They separate exactly when the first Steihaug iterate lands on
the boundary to within rounding, and that is a bookkeeping event rather than a
property of the spectrum. Table~\ref{tab: reconcile} reports both alongside the
two diagnostics.

\section{The angle against the radius}\label{sec: angle}
% =============================================================================

For truncated conjugate gradient on the truncated branch, $1 - \cos(s_k,-g_k)$
is identically zero. Not small, zero. That is the contrast this section is
about.

For the exact solver we fitted the exponent rather than assuming it. Fitting
$\log(1-\cos)$ on $\log\Delta_k$ over the cap sweep gives slope
\FitDeltaSlope\ with $R^2 = \FitDeltaRsq$. The fit is poor and the exponent is
not two.

The reason is that $\Delta_k$ is not the parameter the expansion is in. Write
$s^* = -(H+\sigma I)^{-1}g$ in the eigenbasis of $H$ and expand for large
$\sigma$, using $\sigma \sim \norm{g}/\Delta$. This gives
\begin{equation}
  1 - \cos(s^*, -g) \;\sim\; \tfrac12 \operatorname{Var}_w(\lambda)\,\mu^2 ,
  \qquad \mu = \frac{\Delta}{\norm{g}} ,
  \label{eqn: expansion}
\end{equation}
where $w_i$ are the weights of $g$ on the eigenvectors of $H$ and
$\operatorname{Var}_w$ is the weighted variance of the spectrum. The expansion
parameter is $\mu$, not $\Delta$.

That distinction is exactly what a cap enforces. $\Rgrad(\omega,\overline{\mu})$
sets $\Delta_k = \mu_k\norm{g_k}$ and pins $\mu_k$ at $\overline{\mu}$, so
$\mu_k$ is constant along a run. The radius goes to zero only because
$\norm{g_k}$ does. No exponent in $\mu$ can be fitted from one run, and the
variation has to come from pooling runs at different caps.

Fitting \eqref{eqn: expansion} that way, over \FitMuN\ iterations with
$\mu \leq 0.5$, gives slope \FitMuSlope\ with $R^2 = \FitMuRsq$ and intercept
\FitMuInter\ against the predicted $\log_{10}(1/2) = \FitMuPred$. The second
order is confirmed. Figure~\ref{fig: angle} shows both fits.

The residual decrease of $1-\cos$ along a run therefore comes from
$\operatorname{Var}_w(\lambda)$ and not from $\Delta_k$. The iterates approach
the degenerate origin along a direction on which the Hessian is nearly singular,
the gradient aligns with an eigenvector, and the weighted variance collapses.
The near-eigenvector effect is real. It drives the exact solver's angle to zero.
It is not what separates the two Cauchy statistics of
Section~\ref{sec: cauchy}.

\section{Scope}\label{sec: scope}
% =============================================================================

With the exact Hessian these runs end at the degenerate origin, where
$\nabla^2 f$ is singular. Part~II's inactivity theory assumes a positive
definite Hessian at the limit. An activity statistic collected on such a run is
therefore not evidence about inactivity. The constraint stays active there for a
reason that has nothing to do with the cap. We report the activity column in
Table~\ref{tab: limits} and draw no inactivity reading from it.

Which critical point a run reaches, and why the exact Hessian is the model that
lands on the degenerate one, is a separate question. We record what happens and
do not investigate it here.
